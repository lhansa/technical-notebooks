A p-value of 0.05 leaves your null with at least a 29% chance of being true

bayes
probability
A p-value isn’t the probability that the null is true. You can still bound that probability without picking an effect size, and at p = 0.05 the bound is far from 5%.

Your test gives \(p = 0.05\) and someone in the meeting says there’s a 95% chance the effect is real.

That number is wrong. And you can’t swap in the right one without choosing an alternative hypothesis. Nobody in that meeting is going to choose one.

Or maybe you don’t need to.

What the p-value is about

The p-value is computed assuming the null is true. It’s the probability of seeing data at least this extreme if nothing is going on.

The person in the meeting wants the opposite. They want the probability that nothing is going on, given the data they saw.

Those two are different conditional probabilities. If you play in La Liga, you’re quite likely to be Spanish. If you’re Spanish, you’re very unlikely to play in La Liga.

To go from one to the other you need Bayes. And Bayes asks for two things the p-value doesn’t have: a prior probability for the null, and some idea of how the data behaves when the null is false.

The prior is the easy part. Say 50/50. That’s already generous to the effect.

In most A/B testing programmes, far fewer than half of the ideas move the metric. When real effects are rare, a significant result convinces less, for the same reason a rare positive class wrecks the precision of a good classifier.

The alternative is the hard part. “There is an effect” doesn’t say how big. Each possible size gives a different answer.

Thomas Sellke, M. J. Bayarri and James Berger found a way around it. Their 2001 paper in The American Statistician does something I like a lot: it gives the alternative every advantage it could ask for, and checks how far the null can fall.

How the p-value behaves on each side

Think of the p-value itself as the data.

If the null is true, the p-value is uniform between 0 and 1. A \(p\) of 0.05 is as likely as a \(p\) of 0.73. Its density is flat, equal to 1 everywhere.

If the null is false, small p-values are more likely than big ones. The density piles up near zero. How much it piles up depends on how big the effect is and how much data you have.

The Bayes factor of the null is the ratio between those two densities, evaluated at the p-value you got:

\[B(p) = \frac{f_0(p)}{f_1(p)} = \frac{1}{f_1(p)}\]

If \(B\) is 1, the data doesn’t help you choose. If it’s below 1, the data points towards the alternative. With 50/50 prior odds, the posterior probability of the null is \(B / (1 + B)\).

So the question becomes: how big can \(f_1(p)\) get at \(p = 0.05\)? The bigger it is, the smaller \(B\), and the worse the null looks.

Rig the game for the alternative

You don’t know \(f_1\). Pick a whole family of plausible shapes instead, and let the alternative choose the one it likes best.

Sellke, Bayarri and Berger use densities of the form

\[f_1(p) = \xi \, p^{\xi - 1}, \qquad 0 < \xi < 1.\]

This is a Beta(\(\xi\), 1). Every member decreases from left to right, which is what you’d expect: under an alternative, small p-values should be more common. Values of \(\xi\) close to 1 are almost flat, a tiny effect. Small values of \(\xi\) pile everything near zero, a huge effect.

Now be unfair. For the p-value you observed, pick the \(\xi\) that makes \(f_1(p)\) as large as possible.

Take logs, \(\log \xi + (\xi - 1)\log p\), and set the derivative in \(\xi\) to zero:

\[\frac{1}{\xi} + \log p = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \xi = -\frac{1}{\log p}.\]

For \(p = 0.05\) that gives \(\xi \approx 0.33\). Plug it back in and the Bayes factor simplifies to

\[B(p) \geq -e \, p \log p, \qquad p < 1/e.\]

In words: the evidence for the null can’t be smaller than \(e\) times \(p\) times \(-\log p\).

The condition \(p < 1/e\) matters. Above that point the best \(\xi\) would be bigger than 1, and that shape doesn’t decrease, so it’s out of the family. The best the alternative can do there is a flat line, a tie.

This is the best case for the alternative within that whole family. You didn’t have to choose an effect size. The calculation chose the one that hurts the null most, at the p-value you actually got.

The numbers

At \(p = 0.05\), the bound gives \(B \geq 0.41\). With 50/50 prior odds, the posterior probability of the null is at least \(0.41 / 1.41\), which is 29%.

Here it is for a few p-values:

p-value Smallest Bayes factor of the null Smallest probability that the null is true
0.10 0.63 38%
0.05 0.41 29%
0.01 0.13 11%
0.005 0.072 6.7%
0.001 0.019 1.8%

Look at the first row. A p-value of 0.10 is still sold as “marginally significant” in plenty of reports. In the best case, it leaves you at 38% against 62%. That’s hardly better than flipping a coin.

At 0.05 the odds against the null are at most 2.5 to 1. To get odds of around 14 to 1, you need \(p = 0.005\).

These are lower bounds. With a realistic alternative, or a prior that gives the null more than half, the null comes out stronger.

The family matters, though. Allow any decreasing shape at all, however absurd, and the floor drops to about 5%. That alternative spreads all its weight evenly between zero and the p-value you got. The Beta family is the paper’s line between a generous alternative and one built after looking at the data.

Why this matters to you

Next time someone turns \(p = 0.04\) into “96% sure”, you have a number to put on the table. At least 26% that nothing happened, and that’s the estimate most generous to the effect.

It also changes what you do with a result near the threshold. You don’t ship it as a fact. You treat it as a reason to run the test again, with more data.

And if you’re the one setting the threshold, you now know what 0.05 buys you. At most 2.5 to 1. Decide if that’s enough before you see the data, because after you see it, it always looks like enough.
